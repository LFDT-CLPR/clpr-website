<Section id="why" page="developers">

<Callout kind="note" title="A snapshot of a moving specification">
The specification and its implementations are still changing. This page describes the commits listed in the provenance footer; field names, wire details and parameters in the figures and simulations may move. The specification is the reference.

CLPR, the Cross Ledger Protocol, passes messages between independent ledgers: arbitrary bytes from an application on one ledger to an application on another, delivered asynchronously and in order, with exactly one reply per DATA message. It adds no intermediary network, no bridge validator set and no token. Each ledger verifies the other's state proofs directly, through the channel's verifier contract, so users trust the two ledgers, their CLPR Services and the channel's verifier. Endpoints carry the bundles but cannot forge messages, because every bundle is proven; at least one honest endpoint on each side keeps messages moving.

In one paragraph: an application calls its CLPR Service, naming a channel, a connector, a target application and the data. The connector authorizes the message, and the CLPR Service appends it to the channel's outbound queue, gives it the next message id and extends the queue's running hash. CLPR Endpoints of the two ledgers open an endpoint sync session over gRPC. Each side reports the last message it has received and gets a bundle of the messages after it, which the receiving endpoint submits to its own ledger in an ordinary transaction. The destination CLPR Service passes the bundle and the channel's stored trust anchor to the channel's verifier contract, then checks the message ids and the running hash itself. It calls the application with a gas limit, charges the connector under the recommended design for the execution plus a margin paid to the bundle submitter, and enqueues a REPLY. The REPLY travels back the same way. The simulation in <SecRef id="journey" /> is the same twenty-eight-step round trip as the general page's, with the protocol wording under each step and a source line per step.

Choosing a channel is part of the application's design. Several channels may connect the same two ledgers, for example with verifiers that accept different finality, and the application chooses one at send time. Lower finality gives lower latency and a risk of reorganization. Final or safe state is recommended; a channel whose verifier accepts anything earlier is used at its users' own risk.

CLPR carries ordered, proven bytes from an application on one ledger to an application on another, with exactly one reply per DATA message. What the bytes mean is up to the applications at each end.

<Section id="model" page="developers">

Removing the intermediary does not remove trust. An application that sends a message from Ledger A to Ledger B trusts:

<TrustChainExposure />

1. **Its own ledger's consensus**: Ledger A orders and finalizes correctly.
2. **The local CLPR Service**: the implementation on A executes the protocol faithfully.
3. **The channel's verifier contract**, to accept only proofs of state the peer ledger committed. A malicious or faulty verifier can return fabricated bundle content and so compromise the channel.
4. **The peer ledger's consensus and CLPR Service.**
5. **The remote application.**

Two roles sit outside that chain. The **CLPR Service administrator** configures the Service, admits endpoints where admission is curated and closes channels; it cannot open channels or register connectors, and it has no economic part. The **endpoint operator** runs CLPR Endpoints that serve every channel of its ledger, pays the transaction fees and earns the margin on executed messages.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
Channel creation is permissionless and there is no allow-list of verifiers. Anyone can open a channel with any verifier contract, and a channel's existence does not show that its peer is the intended ledger, because chain ids are self-asserted. Before using a channel, an application checks its verifier, including whether it is upgradeable and who controls the upgrades, and the finality it accepts. Connector operators, whose stake is at risk, vet the verifier before staking on the channel.
</Callout>

**Endpoints are untrusted for safety, required for liveness.** They cannot forge messages, because every bundle is proven, but availability needs at least one honest endpoint on each side; without one, messages stall. A connector, likewise, cannot forge or alter messages. It can refuse to pay, which makes its messages fail on the destination, and slashing its stake is the recommended response.

<SafetyVsLiveness />

<Callout kind="key">
Removing the intermediary does not remove trust. An application trusts its own ledger, its CLPR Service, the channel's verifier, the remote ledger and its service, and the remote application.
</Callout>

<Section id="api" page="developers">

The application surface is one call to send and two callbacks to receive.

<SendAndTwoCallbacks />

To send, an application calls its CLPR Service with the channel, the connector, the target application and the message data, and gets back the message id. To receive, it implements two callbacks: one for an incoming DATA message, whose returned data becomes the reply, and one for the REPLY to a message it sent. Each ledger's Ledger Specifications define the exact interface, its return convention and its execution budget; Hiero and the EVM ledgers use the application interface of the <a href={pinned('clpr-spec', 'ledgers/evm/ledger-family.md')}>EVM family</a>.

On EVM ledgers the application is called with at most the ledger's per-message gas limit, reduced to what the connector can pay for. A returned value becomes the reply data with status `SUCCESS`, and a revert becomes `APPLICATION_ERROR` with the revert data as the reply. Hiero answers a failed call with `APPLICATION_ERROR` and empty reply data. Delivering a reply to the sender is best effort on both: on EVM ledgers the reply callback reaches only a contract, and on Hiero it is made only if the source connector can prepay its gas.

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="Gotchas that cost people time">
- **Check that a callback comes from the CLPR Service.** The callbacks are public functions, so anyone can call them directly; on Hiero every callback comes from the CLPR Service's own address.
- **`target_application` is raw address bytes.** On EVM ledgers and on Hiero it is the 20-byte address. ABI-encoding the address pads it to 32 bytes, the destination finds no application, and the message is answered with `APPLICATION_ERROR`.
- **On EVM ledgers, replies reach contracts only.** Route sends through a contract if you need the reply callback.
- **`channel_id` and `connector_id` are configuration, not constants.** Be ready to move to a new channel when the peer ledger's proof scheme changes.
- **Every payload is untrusted input**, in both directions, even though CLPR provides its authenticity and integrity.
- **Nothing is confidential.** Payloads are stored in plaintext on both ledgers. Encrypt at the application layer if you need confidentiality.
- **Choose the channel by the finality its verifier accepts.** Final or safe state is recommended; a channel that accepts earlier state is faster and is used at its users' own risk.
</Callout>

**Reply statuses.** There are four: `SUCCESS`, `APPLICATION_ERROR`, `CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND` and `CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED`. The last two are attributable to the source connector, and slashing is the recommended response to either; the table is in <SecRef id="connectors" />.

<Callout kind="key">
A send returns a message id; that id is what correlates the eventual reply with the message you sent.
</Callout>

<Section id="queue" page="developers">

Each side of a channel has one outbound queue. DATA, REPLY and CONTROL messages are appended to it in one stream, share one message id sequence and one running hash, and travel in the same bundles.

A **DATA** message carries application content: a connector id, a target application, the sender and opaque message data. The sender is the address of the caller that sent the message, set by the source CLPR Service and never by the caller. A **REPLY** reports the outcome of one DATA message with a status (`SUCCESS`, `APPLICATION_ERROR`, `CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND` or `CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED`) and the application's response data. A **CONTROL** message carries protocol management; its only type is the configuration update, ConfigUpdate. Each queue entry is stored with the running hash after its message.

A message is identified by its channel id and message id, and the recommended Service design keeps queue entries in a store of their own under that key. Acknowledged REPLY and CONTROL messages are deleted; DATA messages stay until their REPLY arrives (see <SecRef id="ordering" />).

<QueueWindow />

### The running hash

Each message's running hash is computed from the previous running hash and the message, starting from the same fixed value on both sides. Every ledger encodes a message in one data format, so both sides hash the same bytes and the receiver can recompute the chain exactly.

<RunningHash id="running-hash-dev" detail />

### Enqueue

The CLPR Service rejects the call, with no change to state, if:

1. the channel does not exist or is not ACTIVE;
2. the connector is not registered on the channel;
3. the connector does not authorize the message;
4. the message's payload is larger than the peer's payload limit; or
5. the outbound queue would then hold more unacknowledged messages than the local queue depth allows, counting any ConfigUpdate enqueued with it.

A Service may apply further limits, such as per-connector queue quotas. Otherwise it enqueues a due ConfigUpdate first, builds the DATA message with the sender set to the caller's address, appends it with the next message id, extends the running hash and returns the message id. The application uses that id to match the eventual REPLY.

A rejected send changes no state. In the recommended economics, the sender pays only the native transaction fee of the send; there is no protocol fee at send.

<Callout kind="note" title="Lazy configuration propagation">
The recommended Service design stores a configuration change once and enqueues nothing at that moment. Each ACTIVE channel enqueues the due ConfigUpdate the next time it sends a message or processes a bundle, so a change costs the same however many channels exist. Because the ConfigUpdate takes a position in the queue fixed by consensus, both ledgers agree on the point in the message stream at which the new configuration takes effect. A ConfigUpdate carries the configuration only; endpoint manifest changes travel in bundles. A receiver rejects the whole bundle if a CONTROL message has no type set or an unknown one.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
Three message types, one id sequence, one hash chain. A receiver rejects the whole bundle when it meets a CONTROL message it does not recognize.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="bundles" page="developers">

Messages travel in bundles. A **bundle** is the proven state of one side of a channel, usually with a contiguous run of messages from its outbound queue. Its proof covers the sender's channel state and the queue entry of the last message in the run, with that message's running hash. The receiver recomputes the chain over every earlier message in the run and compares, so the run is bound in order with nothing omitted. Its channel state, last message and manifest are proven from one state of the source ledger.

Every bundle that carries messages also carries the sender's **queue metadata**: its proven queue position, how far it has received the other side's messages, which the destination takes as its acknowledgement, and its channel status.

<BundlePrefixAnchor />

The run starts right after the last message the receiving side reports it has received, and may be empty. A **partial bundle** ends before the sender's latest message, so a backlog larger than one bundle can carry drains over several. The destination skips messages at the start of a run that it already has, and rejects a run that leaves a gap. A bundle with no messages is valid when it makes progress: a trust anchor change, a manifest update, an acknowledgement or a status change can travel alone.

### Progress criteria

A bundle makes progress if at least one of these holds, evaluated against the channel's state before the bundle. The CLPR Service rejects a bundle that meets none of them.

An endpoint is recommended to evaluate these against its last known view of the destination channel before submitting, and to preview the submission; the CLPR Service evaluates them on chain after the verifier returns. A bundle for a CLOSED channel is rejected before progress is considered.

**Sizing.** Each ledger publishes its own limits on the messages in one bundle and on a bundle's proof size, and the sending endpoint stays within the peer's. A ledger is recommended to set its message cap low enough that a full bundle fits its transaction size and execution limits. The payload limit is checked on both ends: the source rejects an oversized DATA message at send against the peer's limit, and the destination rejects a bundle that carries one against its own.

<Callout kind="key">
One proof per bundle, over the sender's channel state and the last message of the run; the running hash extends it back over every earlier message in the run.
</Callout>

<Section id="verify" page="developers">

This is the algorithm at the centre of the protocol: what a CLPR Service does when a bundle is submitted to it. Any caller can submit one. Step through it on a concrete bundle, then inject faults and watch which step rejects.

<VerifierVsService />

<BundleVerify />

The algorithm runs fourteen steps in one atomic transaction: pre-checks, verification, limits, progress, the manifest and trust anchor, the replay defence, the running hash, the acknowledgement check, response ordering, the acknowledgement, auto-resume, configuration propagation, dispatch, and the status transitions.

The division of labour matters. The **verifier** answers one question: is this bundle authentically the peer's state under the current trust anchor, and if so, what queue metadata, messages, last message id and running hash, manifest and trust anchor change does it carry? Everything else, from the limits, progress, replay and the running hash to the acknowledgement, response ordering, dispatch and status, is the **CLPR Service's own logic** and is the same for every proof system. The Service's checks of message ids, running hash and reply order catch verifier bugs, not a compromised verifier.

Two ordering rules inside the algorithm are easy to miss. The manifest update and the trust anchor change (step 5) take effect before any message is processed, so every message is attributed to the authority that proved it. And a due ConfigUpdate (step 12) is enqueued after the acknowledgement and before any REPLY that the bundle's dispatch enqueues, which fixes its position in the stream.

Processing is atomic: a rejected bundle changes nothing and costs the submitter its transaction fee. On an ACTIVE or PAUSED channel, a bundle with a response ordering violation is not rejected: no message is dispatched, an ACTIVE channel becomes PAUSED, and the manifest and trust anchor updates still take effect (see <SecRef id="ordering" />). Inside an accepted bundle, a failure of a connector or an application affects only that message's REPLY; the other messages are processed normally.

<Callout kind="key">
The verifier answers one question, whether this is authentically the peer's state under the current anchor; every other check belongs to the service and runs the same way for every proof system.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="ordering" page="developers">

CLPR distinguishes two confirmations. An **acknowledgement** is transport-level: the peer's proven statement, in the queue metadata of a bundle, of how far it has received this side's messages. A **reply** is application-level: the REPLY the destination enqueues when it processes a DATA message. Because each side processes the peer's messages in id order and answers each DATA message as it processes it, REPLY messages arrive in the order of the DATA messages they answer.

<TwoConfirmations />

The source checks every REPLY it receives: it must answer the oldest DATA message in the outbound queue that has no REPLY yet, skipping REPLY and CONTROL entries. The first REPLY on a channel answers the first DATA message, which need not have id 1, because a CONTROL message can precede it. Hence the lifecycle rules: acknowledged REPLY and CONTROL messages are deleted from the outbound queue, while **DATA messages are kept past acknowledgement** and deleted when their REPLY is processed, because the order check runs against them.

<Ordering />

A REPLY that does not match is a **response ordering violation**. It shows that the peer's outbound queue is inconsistent, which resending cannot repair. The bundle is processed only up to the order check: nothing is dispatched, and the received message id and running hash do not move, but the manifest and trust anchor updates take effect, and a Service may also apply the acknowledgement; on a CLOSING or DRAINED channel it may reject the bundle instead. An ACTIVE channel becomes **PAUSED**: applications cannot send, queued outbound messages keep syncing, and the first bundle accepted without a violation returns the channel to ACTIVE, which requires the peer to correct its queue. PAUSED is reserved for this case; a bundle that fails verification or any other check is rejected and does not change the channel's status.

<Callout kind="key">
Acknowledged means received; answered means processed. A DATA message leaves the queue only when its REPLY has arrived, in order.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="identity" page="developers">

Each CLPR Service publishes one **ledger configuration**: its identity, the address of its CLPR Service, the limits it imposes on peers and the material a verifier needs to start trusting it.

It holds the protocol version the CLPR Service implements, the ledger's chain id, the address of its CLPR Service, the consensus time of its last change, its throttles, and an initial trust anchor with an id for it where the ledger's proof schemes take the anchor from the configuration. Only the ledger's own CLPR Service administration changes it.

**Identity is a compound key.** A chain id is a CAIP-2 identifier, but it is not unique to one CLPR Service: a ledger runs at least one, and exactly one on Hiero. A peer CLPR Service is therefore identified by its chain id and its service address. Public networks are recommended to use a registered CAIP-2 namespace, and private networks may assign their own. The chain id is self-asserted: any ledger can publish any chain id, and trust in a peer comes from the channel's verifier and trust anchor, not from the chain id.

<CompoundPeerIdentity />

**Protocol version.** The version increases with every change to the Protocol that breaks interoperability, and a new CONTROL message type comes only with a new version. Both sides of a channel must use the same version. A CLPR Service rejects a bundle carrying a ConfigUpdate with a different version, so messaging on that channel stops. Channel setup does not check the peer's version; whether it should is an open issue.

**Throttles** are ledger-wide limits: the same values apply to every peer and every channel, and each channel enforces them independently. A ledger publishes seven: the messages in one bundle, the largest DATA or REPLY payload, the execution budget for one message, the unacknowledged messages a channel's outbound queue may hold, the size of a bundle's proof bytes, how many endpoints its own manifest lists, and how many of a peer's endpoints it keeps. Each channel keeps a copy of the peer's throttles, set at channel setup and replaced by each ConfigUpdate. The sending endpoint stays within the peer's limits; the source checks the peer's payload limit and its own queue depth when a message is sent; and the destination checks its own limits when it processes a bundle.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
A ledger's limit on a bundle's proof size must be at least its largest payload plus the overhead of delivering one message: proof material, queue metadata and framing. Otherwise a message of the largest size can never be delivered and the channel stops. A bundle holds a full count of largest-size messages only if the limit also covers all of them plus the proof; with a smaller limit, the sending endpoint puts fewer large messages in each bundle.
</Callout>

**Strictness.** Limits are published so that a peer is never penalized for a reason it cannot determine from the published configuration. A receiver of a message payload, a channel status or an endpoint sync message rejects one with an unrecognized field, enumeration value or message type, or with malformed content, rather than skipping it. Default values are deployment choices, with recommended values in the Recommendations. The recommended Service design also has an enable flag, checked on every call, as a kill switch.

<Callout kind="key">
A peer CLPR Service is its chain id and its service address: the chain id names the ledger it claims to be, the address names which service on it.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="channels" page="developers">

A **channel** connects a CLPR Service on one ledger with a CLPR Service on another. It exists as a pair of records, one on each ledger, joined by a channel id that is the same on both. Within one Service, a channel id names at most one channel.

Each record holds the channel's view of the peer: its chain id, configuration timestamp and throttles, the verifier binding, the channel context, the trust anchor and its id, and the peer manifest. Part of the record is **shared channel state**, which the peer proves or consumes: the channel id, the status, the version of the peer manifest this side holds, the id of the trust anchor it holds for the peer, and the queue counters (next message id, sent running hash, acknowledged and received message ids, received running hash).

The peer learns this side's status and queue position from the queue metadata of its bundles, proven from that shared state; the rest of the record stays internal to the CLPR Service.

<ChannelVsSyncdata />

**Initial values.** A new channel has sent, acknowledged and received nothing, and both running hashes start from the same fixed value.

### Registration: commit, then reveal

The same channel id is used on both ledgers, so it is registered on each in two phases. The commit phase reserves the channel on both ledgers without revealing the channel id or the key; the reveal phase creates the channel. Registration is open to any caller. The ownership commitment binds the channel id to the registrant's key on one ledger, as a hash of the two; the message signed, the signature scheme and the key encoding are ledger-specific. The registrant chooses the channel id, and a ledger may constrain how it is derived, so the registrant picks an id that satisfies both ledgers. It may use a different key pair on each ledger, unless those constraints require the same key.

<CommitRevealRegistration />

<CommitReveal />

At the reveal, the registrant presents the channel id, its public key, a signature proving control of the key, the verifier contract for the peer ledger, a proof of the peer's configuration and, optionally, a proof of its endpoint manifest. The CLPR Service checks the commitment and the signature, then has the verifier check the configuration proof. The verifier returns the peer's chain id and service address, its configuration timestamp and throttles, the initial trust anchor and its id, and the peer's endpoint manifest, or no manifest when no manifest proof was supplied; an empty endpoint list is valid. The Service stores these values as the channel's initial view of the peer, builds the channel context from the channel id and the peer's service address, and creates the channel as ACTIVE. The registrant has no authority over the channel once it exists: its key serves only to coordinate registration across the two ledgers and, on a ledger that derives the channel id from the key, to derive the id.

<CompleteChannelStores />

Each ledger's Ledger Specifications bind these rules. An EVM ledger, for example, derives the channel id from both chain ids, the registrant's key and a salt, which is why a channel between two EVM ledgers uses the same key on both.

### Lifecycle

<ChannelLife detail />

A channel side has six statuses. **PENDING** is a registered commitment with no channel record yet; **ACTIVE** is normal operation; **PAUSED** follows a response ordering violation; **CLOSING**, **DRAINED** and **CLOSED** take both sides through an orderly close.

The subtleties are in the closing states. A side enters CLOSING by an administrative close, from ACTIVE or PAUSED, or when a bundle shows that the peer is closing. Applications can no longer send, but bundles are processed normally: the peer's DATA messages are delivered and answered, REPLY messages are delivered and acknowledgements advance. CLOSING becomes DRAINED when the outbound queue holds no DATA message, that is, when every DATA message this side sent has been answered; REPLY messages it generated for the peer can still be unacknowledged. DRAINED becomes CLOSED when a bundle shows that the peer is DRAINED or CLOSED, that the peer has acknowledged every message of this side, and that this side has received every message in the peer's proven queue. The last condition is needed because a partial bundle can end before the peer's latest message.

The peer's status travels in the queue metadata of its bundles, which is how one side's close reaches the other; there is no separate close notification. If no such bundle reaches a DRAINED side, the administrator's **recovery close** ends it, provided every message of this side has been acknowledged. PAUSED is different in kind: it is entered automatically on a response ordering violation and left automatically by the first bundle accepted without one; the administrator cannot resume it, only close it.

<TwoSidedClose />

**Verifier binding.** The verifier binding is fixed for the channel's life, so applications can evaluate a channel's verifier once. If the peer ledger's proof scheme changes so that the verifier can no longer verify its bundles, a new channel with a new verifier is registered and applications move to it. A fixed binding does not stop the verifier being an upgradeable contract: check who controls its upgrades.

**Several channels per pair.** Several channels may connect the same two CLPR Services, each with its own channel id, verifier and message queues, and each independent of the others: for instance one per finality level a verifier accepts. Applications choose one at send time.

<Callout kind="key">
A channel's status and queue counters are proven to the peer in the queue metadata of its bundles.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="endpoints" page="developers">

A **CLPR Endpoint** is a node that exchanges bundles with endpoints of the peer ledger and submits the bundles it receives to its own ledger. Endpoints find each other only through endpoint manifests, of which there are two kinds.

The **local manifest** is CLPR Service state, separate from the ledger configuration: the list of the Service's endpoints, a version that starts at 1 and increases by exactly 1 with each change to the set, and the address of the Service it belongs to. Every endpoint in it serves all of the Service's channels, and the list may be empty. An entry holds an optional network address, a numeric IP address and port that an endpoint which only initiates syncs, for example on a private network, leaves out; an optional TLS certificate, present when the endpoint serves sync over TLS; and an optional account identifier of its operator.

How endpoints get into the local manifest depends on the ledger:

- **Hiero:** every consensus node is a CLPR Endpoint, and the manifest is derived from the node roster with no administrator operations. Each node publishes its own endpoint, and a change in node weights alone does not change the manifest. A misbehaving node is removed through the ledger's own governance of its roster.
- **EVM ledgers**, where any party can offer an endpoint: an endpoint registers, and the administrator admits it to the manifest. Endpoints are not slashed; a misbehaving endpoint is removed from the manifest.

The **peer manifest** is held per channel: a proven copy of the peer Service's manifest, and the only source of peer endpoints the specification defines. It is installed at channel setup from a manifest proof checked by the channel's verifier, or starts as no manifest when none was supplied. Whenever a bundle proves a manifest with a higher version, the Service replaces the copy as a whole, before any message of the bundle is processed; a manifest that is not newer is ignored without rejecting the bundle. A copy longer than the ledger's limit on peer endpoints is truncated, in manifest order. An empty peer manifest is valid: this side's endpoints then cannot initiate syncs and wait for the peer's endpoints to initiate them.

<Callout kind="note" title="Manifest updates bypass the queue">
Manifest updates travel in bundles, never through the message queue: a ConfigUpdate carries no endpoint manifest changes. A Service's manifest reaches its peers only as proven content: at channel setup, and in any bundle whose sender sees that the peer's copy is older than its own, as the peer reports in sync requests and queue metadata. Bundle submission is open to any caller, so when every endpoint a channel knows for its peer has changed, anyone who obtains from the peer ledger a bundle carrying its current manifest can submit it on the local ledger. A bundle that carries only the manifest update makes progress. This is recovery scenario R1.
</Callout>

<TwoManifests />

<ProvenManifest />

**Bundle submission is open to any caller** and unrelated to manifest membership. Whoever submits a bundle pays its transaction fee and, under the recommended design, receives the execution charges and slash proceeds it produces. On ledgers with endpoint registration, the defence against Sybil endpoints is administrator curation, which places full trust in the administrator, including trust that it will not empty the manifest. Endpoints are recommended to choose sync peers from the peer manifest at random, weighted toward peers that have delivered useful bundles, and to prefer peers that reciprocate. The weighting is each endpoint's own; the Service keeps no endpoint reputation.

Seen from the endpoint's side, the whole job is a loop: watch the home queue and the peer manifest, open a sync stream for a channel each cycle, ask for what is missing, preview what arrives, submit only what would pass.

<PullNotPush />

<EndpointLoop />

<Callout kind="key">
On EVM ledgers a seat on the endpoint manifest is the administrator's to grant; endpoints are never slashed, and anyone may submit a bundle.
</Callout>

<Section id="sync" page="developers">

Endpoint sync is the one concrete wire protocol of CLPR. Every endpoint that advertises a network address serves one bidirectional streaming gRPC method; an endpoint that advertises none only initiates. Each message on the stream names the channel and may carry a **bundle request**, a **bundle response**, or both. A request describes the requestor's own state for the channel: the last message it has received, its status, the id of the trust anchor its ledger holds, and the version of the sender's manifest its ledger holds. A response carries one bundle, opaque to endpoint sync.

A **sync cycle** is one stream, for one channel, in four steps. The initiator sends its request; the responder answers with its own request and a bundle built for the initiator's; the initiator sends a bundle built for the responder's request, or a terminal message; and the responder ends with a terminal message. Each side sends at most one bundle per cycle, and each cycle uses a new stream; the recommended design lets the connection and its TLS session persist across cycles. A bundle's run of messages starts right after the last message the requestor reports. If the request claims a message the sender has not enqueued, the sender either starts after its own acknowledged position or sends no messages. Submission is not part of the exchange: the sender learns that its bundle was accepted from the requestor's next request.

<SyncCycle />

<Callout kind="impl">
The reference endpoint serves the stream and dials its peers in plaintext only (<a href={pinned('clpr-endpoint', 'docs/sync-protocol.md')}>its sync protocol notes</a>, <a href={pinned('clpr-endpoint', 'clpr-relay-grpc-client/src/main/java/org/hiero/clpr/relay/grpc/client/ClprEndpointClient.java')}>its gRPC client</a>), so it does not connect over TLS to a peer whose manifest entry carries a certificate, as the transport rules require. It does not authenticate its peer and accepts an inbound bundle on the strength of its proof.
</Callout>

Three properties to internalize. The request is **unproven** and must not cause a state change on either ledger: the receiving ledger's verifier and bundle processing remain the only authorities on channel state. A requestor that overstates what it has received gets a bundle its own ledger rejects for a gap, and one that reports less than the sender's acknowledged position gets a bundle its own verifier rejects; neither changes state. Each bundle is **shaped by the requestor's request** in the same stream, not by a cached view of it. And endpoints are recommended to **check a bundle before submitting it**, for example by a read-only preview, because a rejected bundle still costs the submitter its transaction fee.

**Private networks.** An endpoint on a private network can leave its address out of its manifest entry; it then only initiates.

**Transport.** An endpoint whose manifest entry carries a TLS certificate serves sync over TLS with that certificate, and a client must connect to it over TLS, with no fallback to plaintext; an endpoint without one serves plaintext. No endpoint requires a client certificate. TLS gives confidentiality and authenticates the server only when the client checks its certificate; it never authenticates a bundle, whose integrity and authenticity rest on its proof.

**No close notification.** An endpoint does not initiate a cycle for a channel that is PENDING or CLOSED on its own ledger, and sends no bundle to a requestor that reports CLOSED. A DRAINED peer learns that the other side is CLOSED from the bundle a CLOSED responder may still send in answer to the peer's request.

<PrivateNetworkDial />

<Callout kind="key">
The request is a hint; the bundle is the proof. Nothing a bundle request says can change state on any ledger.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="verifiers" page="developers">

A **verifier contract** implements two read-only functions. One checks a peer's configuration proof once, when a channel is completed; the other checks every submitted bundle under the channel's current trust anchor and returns the bundle content it proves. A verifier keeps no per-channel state: the trust anchor comes in on every call and, when it changes, goes out, and the constant identity of the channel arrives as a channel context naming the channel and the peer's CLPR Service. A verifier may keep global state that belongs to no channel, such as a revocation list. The bundle check binds the bundle to the channel and, where the source ledger can host more than one CLPR Service, to the peer's service address; the running hash, the message ids, replay and response ordering are the Service's checks. Each ledger binds the interface in its own **verifier API**, so read the one for the ledger you build for.

<VerifierIo />

### Trust anchors and rotation

The **trust anchor** is the material a verifier needs to check the peer's proofs, such as a ledger id or a validator set. To the CLPR Service it is opaque bytes, stored with the channel together with an id. It is set by the configuration check at channel setup and changed only by bundle processing, from what the bundle check returns, before any message of that bundle is processed. No administrative operation changes it, though a verifier behind an upgradeable proxy can change what it accepts.

A returned trust anchor is one of two kinds. A **trust anchor change** carries a new id: it records a new signing authority, for example after a validator set change on the peer ledger, and the bundle that carries it verifies under the current anchor and contains a proven commitment to the new one. A change makes progress by itself, so a bundle that carries only a change is valid. A **trust anchor update** keeps the id: it refreshes verifier-internal data such as a cache, does not change which proofs are accepted, and does not make progress alone. A verifier accepts a proof only from an authority in the anchor it is given, or from one proven within the same bundle to succeed it.

How far the first trust anchor can be trusted is an open question: unless the verifier fixes the expected anchor itself, the initial anchor comes from the configuration proof, is accepted on first use, and has to be checked out of band.

<TrustAnchor />

Endpoints keep the peer's trust anchor current. Every bundle request reports the id of the trust anchor the requestor's ledger holds, and the sender never sends a bundle that does not verify under it. When that anchor is behind the sender's ledger, the sender sends a **transition bundle**, one that makes a trust anchor change, instead. An anchor several changes behind catches up one transition bundle per cycle, unless the proof scheme carries several changes in one bundle. A ledger can be used with CLPR only if, whenever its signing authority changes, a proof can be built that the current authority verifies and that commits to the new one.

### Proof schemes and verifier APIs

A **proof scheme** defines what is proven about a bundle's content, how, and how it is verified. A verifier contract combines one proof scheme of the source ledger with the verifier API of the destination ledger. Hiero ledgers produce bundles in two schemes: one for Hiero destinations, checked by a built-in verifier and anchored to the ledger id, and one for EVM destinations, checked by a verifier contract and anchored to the hash of the current hinTS verification key, which changes with the roster. The EVM ledger specified so far, which runs QBFT, has three schemes, which differ in where the validator set comes from: epoch boundary headers, every block header, or a validator contract's storage. The schemes, their trust anchors and who verifies them are listed in the specification's <a href={pinned('clpr-spec', 'ledgers/README.md')}>ledger index</a>.

<TwoStateTrees />

<Callout kind="gotcha">
**The commitment level is the verifier's choice.** It can be final, safe, or an earlier and faster level, so channels with different latency and risk can connect the same ledgers. A verifier is recommended to accept only final or safe state. On a peer ledger without instant finality, a channel whose verifier accepts earlier state is used at its users' own risk: the proven state can be reverted after its messages are delivered.
</Callout>

<PhantomMessage />

<Callout kind="key">
The service stores, passes and replaces the trust anchor and never reads it. It is replaced only from what the channel's verifier returns, and its authority changes only when the verifier accepts a proof, checked against the current anchor, that commits to a new one.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="connectors" page="developers">

A **connector** authorizes the messages applications send on a channel and pays for their execution on the destination ledger. It is registered separately on each ledger of the channel, under a connector id derived from the channel id, the operator's key and a salt, so the same key and salt give the same id on both ledgers. On each ledger a connector is registered on exactly one channel; a channel can have many connectors, and one key can register connectors on many channels. The record names the connector's contract and an admin who can remove it. Execution charges are paid from the connector contract's own balance, and the recommended design adds a locked stake that can be slashed.

Registration mirrors channels: commit, then reveal, and the Service re-derives the connector id and checks the commitment. Because the channel id is an input, registering a connector id on one channel reserves nothing on any other. A connector carries messages only if it is registered on both ledgers, on the same channel and with the same id: a connector registered only on the source authorizes messages that the destination answers with `CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND`, without calling the application.

<BilateralConnector />

### Authorizing

The source CLPR Service calls the connector contract's authorization function before a message is queued, and rejects the send unless it returns true; a failed call counts as a refusal. The connector may apply any logic, such as allow-lists or payment requirements, and the call must not modify CLPR Service state. Returning true is a commitment: the connector commits that its counterpart on the destination ledger can pay for executing the message, and that it can itself cover the cost of handling the reply. That commitment is the basis for slashing.

### Paying

On the destination, each DATA message's connector id is resolved directly to the local connector with the same id on the channel, with no authorization call. If the connector cannot pay, the message is not delivered and is answered `CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED`. Under the recommended economics, the Service charges the connector the execution cost plus a margin, one value for the whole Service, and pay all of it to the account that submitted the bundle; no share goes to the Service or its administrator, except as a fallback when the transfer to the submitter fails. Ledgers differ in when the charge is taken. On Hiero the worst-case charge is collected before the call and unused gas is refunded after it. On EVM ledgers the charge is collected after the call, on the gas it used, in the Service's fee asset: the native currency, or a token fixed when the Service is deployed. Delivering a REPLY to the sending application is also charged to the source connector.

<Money id="money-dev" detail />

### Slashing

Slashing is a Recommendation. It happens on each ledger independently, and each ledger compensates the submitter of the bundle that exposed the failure there.

An underfunded connector is therefore slashed twice: once on the destination ledger, and once on the source ledger when the REPLY arrives. Penalties escalate with each slash and end in a **ban**, which removes the connector from the channel and forfeits its remaining stake. Penalties are paid to the submitter of the bundle being processed, like execution charges. Recommended per-connector queue quotas keep one connector from filling a channel's outbound queue.

Two gaps remain. The destination submitter absorbs the cost of a DATA message whose connector does not exist there. And an endpoint is paid only for bundles it submits to its own ledger, not for building and sending bundles to the peer, so it can receive without sending; preferring peers that deliver bundles is the only mitigation, and how to compensate sending is an open issue.

<Callout kind="open">
**Calibrating the connector stake.** A connector's locked stake has to cover the worst-case losses its messages can cause: on the destination, the execution cost of messages that arrive while it is underfunded; on the source, the penalties for failure replies. If the stake is too small, a connector can authorize a burst of messages that costs endpoints more than slashing recovers. Each CLPR Service enforces a configured minimum stake, which is not derived from the connector's possible exposure.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="key">
A connector authorizes on the sending ledger and pays on the receiving one; in the recommended design, a locked stake on each ledger backs that promise.
</Callout>

<Section id="security" page="developers">

The security considerations below are recommendations of the CLPR Service design, which a deployment may depart from.

- **Verifier choice.** Channel creation is permissionless and there is no allow-list of verifiers; a malicious or faulty verifier can return fabricated bundle content and compromise the channel. Evaluate a channel's verifier before using it, including whether it is upgradeable and who controls the upgrades.
- **Finality.** For a peer ledger without instant finality, a verifier accepts only final or safe state.
- **Endpoint admission.** Where endpoints register, administrator curation is the defence against Sybil endpoints. Endpoints are not slashed or penalized for the bundles they submit: they submit at their own cost and recover it from the margin on accepted bundles, and the transaction fee deters the submission of stale manifest bundles. Curation places full trust in the administrator, including trust that it will not empty the manifest.
- **Reentrancy.** Dispatching a message hands control to another contract. The Service guards its state-changing entry points against reentrancy and completes its own state changes, including debiting charges, before making an external call.

<ChecksEffectsDispatch />

<Callout kind="impl">
The EVM contracts guard bundle submission against reentrancy (<a href={sol('src/logic/BundleLogic.sol')}>the bundle logic</a>), call the application before charging the connector, as the channel economics describe for EVM ledgers, and write the channel's counters after the whole dispatch loop (<a href={sol('src/libraries/service/BundleLib.sol')}>the bundle library</a>). They answer a garbled DATA message with an `APPLICATION_ERROR` reply and carry on with the bundle, where the specification rejects the whole bundle (<a href={pinned('clpr-smart-contracts', 'docs/bundle-flow.md')}>bundle flow notes</a>).
</Callout>

- **Application payloads.** CLPR provides authenticity and integrity but not confidentiality, and payloads are stored in plaintext on both ledgers. Treat every payload as untrusted input, and encrypt it yourself if it must stay confidential.
- **Service upgrades.** On EVM ledgers, peers prove the Service's state by its storage layout, so an upgrade that changes the layout breaks bundle production and verification. Coordinate it with endpoint operators and verifier deployments, or deploy a new Service. The EVM contracts are not upgradeable.
- **Queue monopolization.** Per-connector queue quotas keep one connector from filling a channel's queue.
- **Transport and sync hygiene.** An endpoint serves TLS whenever it has a certificate, and a client verifies the server against the manifest certificate or a configured pin. An endpoint may stop or reduce syncing with a peer that breaks the sync rules, sends bundles its ledger rejects, or holds a stream open without completing it.

Misbehavior detection is strictly local: there is no cross-ledger misbehavior reporting.

<Callout kind="key">
CLPR provides authenticity and integrity, not confidentiality; every payload is untrusted input to the application that receives it.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Architecture id="architecture-dev" detail />

### EVM: `clpr-smart-contracts`

A Foundry project. One immutable router contract holds the CLPR Service's state and dispatches to logic modules sharing one storage layout. The repository publishes the interfaces the protocol calls outward, for <a href={sol('src/interfaces/IClprApplication.sol')}>applications</a>, <a href={sol('src/interfaces/IClprConnector.sol')}>connectors</a> and <a href={sol('src/interfaces/IClprVerifier.sol')}>verifiers</a>, and <a href={sol('src/interfaces/IClprService.sol')}>the service's public interface</a>.

<EvmRouterModules />

The repository records its design choices under `docs/`. The contracts are a reference implementation. The specification's current text was aligned with the implementations, and where an implementation and the specification still disagree, the specification is authoritative: the implementation changes, or a CLIP changes the specification.

### Hiero: the native service

On Hiero the CLPR Service is a native service of the ledger, which applications call at a fixed system contract address, and every consensus node is a CLPR Endpoint. Its state is proven by Merkle paths into the ledger's state, under a threshold signature on the block root. A channel's verifier on Hiero is either a built-in verifier, a system contract that needs no deployment, or a deployed contract that implements the Hiero verifier API; the built-in verifiers check bundles from Hiero ledgers and from EVM ledgers running QBFT.

### The endpoint: `clpr-endpoint`

A standalone Java relay for EVM ledgers. Each cycle the endpoint picks a peer from the peer manifest and opens a sync stream for the channel: it sends a request describing its own state, receives the peer's bundle together with the peer's request, and answers with a bundle built from its own ledger for that request. A bundle it receives, whichever side opened the stream, is decoded and submitted to the local CLPR Service. The stream runs in plaintext, where the specification runs it over TLS whenever the peer's manifest entry carries a certificate, and the endpoint does not authenticate its peer: a bundle stands on its proof.

<Section id="evolution" page="developers">

The specification is governed by CLIP-0001, which defines what it contains, how it is versioned and how it changes. Its normative text is in three categories. The **Protocol** (MUST) defines interoperability across all ledgers, in modules such as messaging, bundle, verifier interface and endpoint sync. The **Ledger Specifications** (MUST for anyone interoperating with that ledger) say how each ledger gathers, proves and verifies bundle content. The **Recommendations** (SHOULD) describe a working deployment, which may depart from them without affecting interoperability. A non-normative overview introduces the parts and the vocabulary, and a separate document lists the known open design questions.

<SpecDocumentMap />

**Versions.** A version is a released, unchanging definition of the Protocol. Before the first major version, versions are numbered v0.1, v0.2, v0.3 and so on, and each is incompatible with the others: implementations interoperate only when they implement the same version. Version 0.2 is final, the baseline under CLIP-0001, with no CLIPs integrated; version 0.3 is open on the main branch. Implementations target a final version, never the main branch. Each Protocol module records the version in which it last changed, and each Ledger Specification document carries its own revision number.

**CLIPs.** Changes to Protocol behaviour are made through CLPR Improvement Proposals. A CLIP tells the story of a change in order: the problem, the approaches considered, the proposed solution, the specification changes it makes, compatibility and migration, and security considerations. It moves from Draft through Review and Last Call to Accepted, is then Scheduled into a version and Integrated into the text, and becomes Final when that version is released; it can also be Withdrawn, Rejected or Deferred. A CLIP is scheduled, integrated and released as a whole. Once it is integrated, the specification text is the definition, and the CLIP stays in the repository as the explanation of how and why the text changed. Other changes, such as a new ledger or proof scheme, a Recommendation or an editorial fix, are made by pull request.

**Who decides.** The specification is developed under the Community Specification License 1.0. Participants open issues, submit pull requests and author CLIPs. Editors keep the text accurate and consistent and give final approval before changes are merged. Maintainers determine consensus, decide which CLIPs go into each version and manage releases; unanimity is preferred but not required.

Two rules govern change on the wire. A change that breaks interoperability raises the protocol version, and both sides of a channel must use the same one. And a message payload, channel status or sync message that a receiver does not recognize is rejected, never skipped.

If you are looking for somewhere to contribute, the open issues are the map: the known design questions, each with its problem, the current behaviour and the options.

<Section id="governance" page="developers">

CLPR is incubated as a lab of **LF Decentralized Trust**, a Linux Foundation project. The signed Labs charter, kept in the lab's governance repository, fixes the rules every lab operates under:

- **Oversight** by the foundation's Technical Advisory Council (TAC), whose oversight can include approving project proposals, including incubation and deprecation, organizing and removing sub-projects, and approving the processes for contributing.
- **Licensing.** Inbound and outbound code under Apache License 2.0, every new inbound code contribution carrying a Developer Certificate of Origin sign-off; specifications under the Community Specification License 1.0; documentation under CC BY 4.0. Exceptions need a two-thirds vote of the entire TAC. Files should carry SPDX identifiers.
- **Copyright** in new contributions stays with its holders, and no contributor is required to assign it. Project trademarks are held for the project by LF Projects, LLC, or an associated hosting entity.
- **Openness.** Participation cannot be refused on any basis that is not reasonable and non-discriminatory, and "the output of all Project discussions, proposals, timelines, decisions, and status should be made open and easily visible to all".

The mission of the Labs project is "to support development of very early open projects". The Hedera Governing Council undertook to donate the CLPR brand and trademark to LFDT once the lab was accepted.

Inside the lab, every team and its rights on each repository are defined in a teams file in the governance repository, and a change to a role or team is a pull request against that file referencing a governance issue. Anyone who contributes is a Contributor and needs no team. Committers are per repository: each code repository has a committers team with write access to it. One maintainers team, with maintain rights on every repository, takes the decisions that affect the whole organization, including creating repositories and adding or removing Committers and Maintainers. General teams cover release engineering, security and foundation staff. Decisions aim for consensus; the Maintainers vote when there is none or when a formal decision, such as adding a Committer or Maintainer, is required, under the quorum and voting period the lab's <a href={pinned('governance', 'GOVERNANCE.md')}>governance document</a> sets.

The lab's scope: collaboration on the specification, the reference implementations of service, verifiers and endpoints, application development, and native integrations into other ledger types.

Plus a few terms used on this page, as the specification defines them: **queue metadata**, the sender's proven queue position, carried in every bundle that carries messages; **partial bundle**, a bundle that ends before the sender's last queued message; **acknowledgement**, a ledger's proven statement that it received messages, transport-level, unlike a reply; **trust anchor change** and **trust anchor update**, a returned trust anchor with a new id, which makes progress, and one with the same id, which does not; **transition bundle**, a bundle that makes a trust anchor change for the requestor, possibly carrying no messages; **proof scheme**, what is proven about bundle content, how, and how it is verified; **verifier API**, a ledger's concrete binding of the verifier interface; **bundle aggregation**, how an endpoint gathers bundle content from its ledger's state; **endpoint sync**, the protocol CLPR Endpoints use to exchange bundles; **CAIP-2**, the `namespace:reference` chain identifier standard that chain ids follow.
